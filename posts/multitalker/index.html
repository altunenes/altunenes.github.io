Overlapping speakers with Parakeet TDT.
Posted on 2026-10-31

Table of Contents

  • Training
  • The numbers
  • In Rust
  • Where it falls short

When two people talk at the same time, speech recognition models write one sentence out of both, or one of them just disappears.

In parakeet-rs the usual way to get speakers is diarization. Nemotron-3 Diarization says who speaks when, and every word goes to whoever was speaking at its timestamp. This works fine until people talk over each other. Then a sentence gets cut between two speakers and the quieter person is lost.

NVIDIA has a model for exactly this, the multitalker Parakeet, but it is English only and made for streaming: it hears small pieces of audio at a time, which is great for live captions. Most of what I transcribe is recordings, and there an offline model that hears the whole sentence is usually more accurate. So I wanted the same thing on parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3, my favorite offline model, which also knows 25 European languages.

The idea

I used NVIDIA's design. The diarization output for one speaker (is this person talking in this 80 ms frame or not) goes into the encoder through a few extra layers, called speaker kernels. Then the model runs once per speaker, and each time it writes down only that person, even when someone else is talking at the same time.

I froze the whole TDT model and trained only the speaker kernels plus small adapters in its 24 encoder layers. That is 7.4M parameters out of 634M. The original model never changes, so one person talking alone should come out as good as before.

Training

Everything trained on a free Colab T4, around 30k steps per round.

The first rounds used FLEURS clips from all 25 languages, mixed on top of each other with 1 to 4 speakers. The model learned to separate voices, but on real meetings it was worse than plain TDT (31.4% vs 24.7% on AMI). It caught the first sentence of a speaker and dropped the later ones. Which makes sense: in a mixed clip every person says one sentence. In a real conversation people speak again and again.

So in round 3 I added real conversations: dialogues built from VoxPopuli speeches, and the AMI training meetings. As labels I used TDT's own transcripts wherever they agreed with the human ones, so the model keeps TDT's writing style.

That fixed it. On the AMI test meetings round 3 got 15.5%, plain TDT 26.5%.

The numbers

All scores are cpWER: word error rate where each real speaker is matched with the best output speaker, lower is better. Both systems get the same Nemotron-3 Diarization output. "Plain TDT" is what parakeet-rs users run today, words given to speakers by their timestamps.

In Rust

The encoder is exported to ONNX with two extra inputs: the speaker's activity and everyone else's. parakeet-rs runs the diarization, cuts the audio into chunks of about 20 s at pauses, and runs TDT once per speaker in each chunk. A 106 s recording of two people takes 13.6 s on my M3 Air, CPU only.

use parakeet_rs::MultitalkerTDT;

let mut model = MultitalkerTDT::from_pretrained(
    "multitalker-tdt-0.6b-v3",
    "nemotron3_diar_v3.onnx",
    Some(config),
)?;
for u in model.transcribe(&audio, 16000, 1)? {
    println!("[{:.2} - {:.2}] speaker_{}: {}", u.start, u.end, u.speaker_id, u.text);
}

Where it falls short

Two or four people at once is fine, eight at once comes out as fragments. It is also only as good as the diarization: if Nemotron misses a speaker, so does this. And since it runs once per speaker, it gets slower than plain TDT when many people talk.

It is offline. It needs the whole chunk, so for live captions NVIDIA's streaming model is the right one.

AMI left a small accent too. Some English output now writes numbers as words ("eighty two") and uses British spelling like "diarisation".

I measured 7 of the 25 languages. The others should follow the base TDT quality, but I haven't tested them.
